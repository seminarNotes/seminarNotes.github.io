This project addresses the problem of replacing conventional PID control with data-driven Model Predictive Control (MPC) in thermal heat-treatment processes. The industrial requirement is to maintain 930 °C for approximately 3 hours, then 880 °C for approximately 1 hour within a tolerance band of ±5–10 °C.

From operational data, we identified a simple yet effective first-order ARX(1,1) model, compensating for modeling discrepancies—including time delays, cold-load effects, and thermal losses— through an Extended State Observer (ESO) that estimates disturbances in real time. The resulting ARX+ESO predictive model is integrated into an MPC framework that explicitly handles input saturation, rate constraints, and temperature bounds. Simulation results on six independent datasets demonstrate that the proposed controller significantly outperforms existing PID schemes across tracking error, energy consumption, and overall cost metrics.

1. Background and Problem Formulation

1.1 Thermal Process Characteristics

Heat treatment is a critical step in materials processing that determines mechanical properties and microstructure. The fundamental challenge is to drive the furnace chamber to a target setpoint $T_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and maintain it with tight tolerance throughout a prescribed dwell period.

In industrial practice, several nonideal phenomena degrade control performance:

  • Cold-load effect: When material is loaded into the chamber, the internal temperature drops abruptly;
  • Thermal losses: Radiative and convective losses cause the system to cool faster than predicted;
  • Input time delay: The heating element response and thermal transmission exhibit latency.

These nonlinearities and uncertainties are difficult to capture in a fixed-gain PID controller. As a result, overshoot, input saturation, and excessive energy consumption are common. Moreover, when the reference trajectory changes in stages and includes long dwell periods, a controller that ignores future information is fundamentally limited. This motivates the use of Model Predictive Control, which exploits an explicit process model and forecasts over a prediction horizon to make decisions that anticipate disturbances and respect hard constraints.

1.2 Motivation for MPC

Classical approaches such as manual tuning or adaptive PID gain scheduling are labor-intensive and often result in conservative parameter choices. In contrast, MPC offers:

  • Explicit handling of input and rate constraints;
  • Predictive horizon for anticipatory control;
  • Systematic incorporation of a disturbance model (ESO) for robustness.

Our goal is to demonstrate that a simple, data-identified model combined with real-time disturbance estimation can outperform entrenched PID solutions while remaining computationally tractable for industrial deployment.

2. System Identification and Modeling

2.1 ARX Model Identification

We model the thermal dynamics using a first-order ARX (AutoRegressive eXogenous) structure:

$$T_{k+1} = a \, T_k + b \, u_k + z_k$$

where $T_k \in [0, 1]$ is the normalized chamber temperature at time step $k$, $u_k \in [0, 1]$ is the normalized heating input, $a, b$ are model parameters identified via least-squares regression on operational data, and $z_k$ is an unmeasured disturbance term capturing model mismatch.

The simplicity of ARX(1,1) offers several advantages:

  • Low computational burden, enabling fast MPC solves;
  • Direct interpretability: $a$ relates to thermal time constant, $b$ to heater efficiency;
  • Sufficient to capture the slow dominant modes of the thermal response.

2.2 Extended State Observer (ESO) for Disturbance Estimation

Rather than attempting to model every physical phenomenon (delays, cold-load transients, etc.), we treat all deviations from the ARX model as an aggregated disturbance $z_k$ and estimate it in real-time using an Extended State Observer:

$$\hat{z}_k = \text{ESO}(T_k, u_k, y_k)$$

where $y_k$ is the measured furnace temperature. The ESO exploits the fact that the disturbance $z_k$ evolves slowly relative to the system time constant, allowing stable real-time reconstruction. By feeding $\hat{z}_k$ into the MPC prediction model, we achieve robustness against model uncertainty without explicitly formulating constraints on the disturbance.

The ESO structure is a reduced-order observer that maintains an internal state estimate $\hat{x}_k$ and corrects it based on measurement residuals. A tuning parameter $l_o$ (observer gain) balances responsiveness to measurement noise versus tracking speed of the disturbance dynamics.

3. MPC Design Framework

3.1 Augmented Prediction Model

The MPC prediction model combines the ARX structure with the estimated disturbance:

$$T_{k+i+1|k} = a \, T_{k+i|k} + b \, u_{k+i} + \hat{z}_{k+i|k}$$

where $T_{k+i|k}$ denotes the predicted temperature $i$ steps ahead given information at time $k$, and $\hat{z}_{k+i|k}$ is a prediction of the disturbance. For simplicity, we assume $\hat{z}_{k+i|k} \approx \hat{z}_k$ (constant disturbance assumption) or use a low-pass extrapolation if recent trends are available.

3.2 Optimal Control Problem

MPC solves a finite-horizon optimal control problem at each time step. The decision variable is the input sequence $\{u_{k}, u_{k+1}, \ldots, u_{k+N-1}\}$ over prediction horizon $N$, and the objective is:

\begin{aligned} J(u_k, \ldots, u_{k+N-1}) = &\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \left[ \beta (T_{k+i+1|k} - T_{\mathrm{ref},k+i})^2 + \lambda (\Delta u_{k+i})^2 + \eta \, u_{k+i}^2 \right] \end{aligned}

The three penalty terms are:

  • Tracking term: $\beta (T_{k+i+1|k} - T_{\mathrm{ref},k+i})^2$ penalizes deviation from the reference trajectory;
  • Rate-of-change term: $\lambda (\Delta u_{k+i})^2$ where $\Delta u_{k+i} = u_{k+i} - u_{k+i-1}$ smooths input transitions to avoid wearing out the heating element;
  • Input magnitude term: $\eta \, u_{k+i}^2$ limits energy consumption and prevents unnecessary operation.

The weights $\beta$, $\lambda$, $\eta$ are tuned to balance performance objectives. Typical values chosen empirically are $\beta = 10$, $\lambda = 1$, $\eta = 0.1$.

3.3 Constraints

The optimization is subject to the following hard constraints:

  • Input bounds: $u_{\min} \leq u_{k+i} \leq u_{\max}$, e.g., $0 \leq u \leq 1$ (normalized);
  • Rate constraints: $|\Delta u_{k+i}| \leq \Delta u_{\max}$, e.g., $\Delta u_{\max} = 0.1$ per time step;
  • Temperature bounds: $T_{\min}(k+i) \leq T_{k+i|k} \leq T_{\max}(k+i)$ where bounds may be stage-dependent (e.g., 850–950 °C during dwell).

The constrained optimization is solved via Sequential Least-Squares Programming (SLSQP) or Quadratic Programming if the problem is reformulated as a QP. Typical solve time is <1 ms on a standard embedded processor.

5. Insights and Lessons Learned

5.1 What Worked Well

The combination of a simple ARX(1,1) model with real-time ESO disturbance estimation proved highly effective. Despite the inherent simplicity, the framework captures the dominant dynamics and compensates for unmodeled phenomena (delays, cold-load effects, thermal losses) through the disturbance feedback loop. This demonstrates that in process control, robustness through feedback is often superior to complexity through modeling.

The MPC formulation explicitly handles input saturation and rate constraints, preventing violations that would otherwise require additional safety layers. This end-to-end integration simplifies deployment and reduces commissioning effort.

5.2 Practical Challenges

Identifying the ARX(1,1) parameters from noisy operational data required careful preprocessing, including outlier removal and low-pass filtering to suppress measurement noise below the control bandwidth. The time-delay estimation was non-trivial, requiring correlation analysis or fitting with delay-augmented model structures.

ESO tuning required empirical iteration; overly aggressive observer gains led to noise amplification, while conservative gains degraded disturbance tracking speed. A mid-range tuning ($l_o \approx 0.3$–0.5) generally performed well across datasets.

5.3 Lessons Learned

1. Data Quality is Paramount: The final MPC performance is limited by the quality and richness of identification data. Datasets rich in excitation (diverse operating points and transient events) yield more robust models than monotonic or quasi-steady datasets.

2. Disturbance Feedback Beats Perfect Models: Rather than investing heavily in accurate first-principles modeling, a simple black-box model combined with adaptive disturbance estimation provides better robustness and is faster to deploy.

3. Constraint Handling is Critical: Many PID-to-MPC transitions fail when constraints are naively ignored. Proper constraint formulation and solver robustness are prerequisites for reliability.

4. Tuning is Problem-Dependent: There is no universal weight set $(\beta, \lambda, \eta)$ that works for all processes. The present study employed weights tuned to balance settling time, overshoot, and energy use; different priorities would require re-tuning. Automated tuning methods (e.g., frequency response-based design or learning-based approaches) remain an open direction for future work.
